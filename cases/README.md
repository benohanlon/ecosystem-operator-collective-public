# Cases

These cases show how independent operators get a network started, how developers and early users adopt what it makes possible, and how communities govern it and organise the work as it grows.

## How we write a case

Each case takes a problem others might recognise and uses one example to show a principle in practice. We explain what made the problem hard, what changed in how people worked together, what each party brought and gained, and what the evidence shows became possible.

## Solving the cold start problem

Cardano set out to build an open financial operating system for people left out of traditional banking. For it to be decentralised, independent operators would have to run the network. That meant asking people to put time and money into running a stake pool before they knew whether they could attract enough delegation to make it pay.

Banking the unbanked gave prospective operators a chance to do some good in the world. Cardano’s commitment to peer-reviewed research appealed to people who wanted to be the adults in the room in a space that prized moving fast and breaking things. Running a stake pool gave them a way to act on those beliefs and a business they could build.

People still needed to get a pool running. Stake Pool School showed them how. Regular office hours and newsletters helped them keep up as the network changed. Input Output’s delegation programme helped selected pools improve their chances of making a profit while they tried to attract delegation of their own. To win that delegation, operators explained Cardano to people who already trusted them. The people who ran the network helped spread the vision that brought them in.

In March 2021, stake pools took over production of every new block on Cardano. By September 2022, more than 3,000 pools were operating.

*Ben O’Hanlon was responsible for Input Output’s delegation programme.*

## Activating developers through education and engagement

Cardano’s smart contract launch could get developers to look at the network. Getting them to build on it was harder. Few knew Plutus, and the tools they would use were still taking shape.

In spring 2021, Input Output’s education team invited developers to learn and test Plutus before its mainnet release. Developers got early access and help with a new language. Input Output got feedback from people trying to build with it.

After smart contracts went live in September, the Cardano Summit gave Pioneers a chance to show what they could build. Engineers from Input Output and partner companies mentored teams on problems other builders faced. The teams submitted 16 projects, including a wallet testing tool. Developers from the early cohorts also shared guides and answered questions on Discord. Input Output helped gather those guides into Plutus Community Docs, giving the next developers a place to start.

## Starting up a decentralised autonomous organisation

Cardano was preparing to hand over decisions about how the network developed and what its treasury funded. The people taking on that authority were spread across countries, languages and organisations. They needed a way to agree the rules and a way to make decisions together once those rules were in place.

Intersect gave the work a member organisation to coordinate through. It and its Civics Committee helped local groups hold 63 workshops. Participants could debate the draft in their own communities and elect delegates to carry their views to the convention. A separate community forum gave voting representatives a place to deliberate and invited Input Output, Intersect and the Cardano Foundation to moderate alongside community members.

The constitution agreed at the convention went to an on-chain vote. In February 2025, 84.23% of active delegated voting stake backed it. By August, the community had used its authority to approve treasury funding for Input Output Engineering’s protocol roadmap. The organisation that had helped build the network now had to make its case to the people governing it.

## Getting early adoption through a network people already trust

RealFi needed its first users to try a new stablecoin on Cardano. That first group could show its peers how the product worked and help RealFi see where people got stuck.

RealFi built its accelerator around Cardano stake pool operators. They already ran infrastructure the community relied on and had relationships with people who chose to delegate their stake to them. RealFi looked at who could organise people online and used Cardano’s delegation records to see which pools people already backed. Delegators could earn points through their pool and by trying the testnet. Operators earned points tied to what the delegators they brought in earned, giving them a reason to help people get started.

PetLoverStake published a walkthrough for its own community, showing people how to delegate and try the product. On 17 July 2026, eleven days after the testnet opened, RealFi reported around 3,000 verified wallets across the testnet. Later that month it reported that delegation had grown for 84% of participating operators and 17% of a comparison group of similarly sized pools it had selected. That short comparison gave operators an early indication of what they could gain by taking part.

## Scaling ecosystem work across independent autonomous teams

**Autonomous teams choose to work together when the work advances something each already values.**

When product launches became autonomous business units at Input Output’s venture studio, each unit had its own priorities and measures of success. A unit could know where it was stuck without knowing who elsewhere could help. The ecosystem team had relationships and experience the unit could draw on. To make those useful, it first had to understand the problem in the unit’s terms.

The Ecosystem Operating System gave units a way to choose help based on what they wanted to achieve. Suppose a unit wants more developers to complete their first integration. The unit brings product knowledge and commits engineering time. The shared team brings specialist experience and partner relationships. Together they choose the services that fit, such as a working example, technical office hours or an integration review. Both judge the work by how many developers get a first integration working.

When several units face that problem, there’s a reason to work together. An outside partner can contribute expertise and get a tested example it can reuse with its own clients. The units get a better way to help developers get started. Each party can see what it would contribute, what it would gain and what they could achieve together.

## Unblocking developers

After Alonzo, application teams building on Plutus could identify the issues that stopped their work. The engineers maintaining Plutus understood the effort and dependencies involved in fixing them. Ben started DApp Orbit to bring those views together. The developer relations team collected feedback and met with engineering each week to help weigh issues and decide what to fix first.

The team's March 2023 retrospective records the active backlog falling from more than 200 issues to 46 and reports that issues which previously took months were being resolved in days. The retrospective is an internal team record. This is the team's backlog and turnaround, and the case above describes Ben's role in setting up the working relationship.

## Building a coalition to fund shared infrastructure

In Cardano’s first funding cycle after moving to full on-chain governance, independent infrastructure teams needed enough certainty to keep people and plan delivery. The people voting needed to understand what they were funding and how it would be checked. CDEC shifted from trying to form a consortium to helping members coordinate funding proposals. Ben led an operating review and published the coalition’s open letter on the costs of delay and the value of milestone-based payments.

NFTCDN illustrates how a member could make its case. It explained its costs and what might happen after the funding period. The Cardano Foundation recorded changing its vote from No to Yes after the clarification. The treasury withdrawal was enacted, and NFTCDN offered free access to developers.

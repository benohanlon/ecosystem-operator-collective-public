# Cases

These cases show how independent operators get a network started, how developers and early users adopt what it makes possible, and how communities govern it and organise the work as it grows.

## How we write a case

Each case takes a problem others might recognise and uses one example to show a principle in practice. We explain what made the problem hard, what changed in how people worked together, what each party brought and gained, and what the evidence shows became possible.

## Reaching early users through communities they already trust

*Asset-based community development*

RealFi needed its first users to try USDrf, a stablecoin designed to give holders access to returns from real-world credit when they stake it. The testnet gave RealFi a way to learn how people used the product and where they needed help.

RealFi built its accelerator around Cardano stake pool operators. They already ran infrastructure the community relied on and had relationships with people who chose to delegate their stake to them. RealFi looked at who could organise people online and used Cardano’s delegation records to see which pools people already backed. Delegators could earn points through their pool and by trying the testnet. Operators earned points tied to what the delegators they brought in earned, giving them a reason to help people get started.

PetLoverStake wrote a walkthrough that explained to its own community how to delegate and try the testnet. It could explain RealFi in terms familiar to the people it already helped. RealFi gained feedback from testers and bug reporters. After the Cardano mainnet launch, its Head of Product credited that work and the operators who brought their delegators with helping the launch go smoothly.

In a short July 2026 comparison, RealFi reported that delegation grew for 84% of participating operators and 17% of similarly sized pools it selected outside the programme. The comparison was observational and illustrates what operators stood to gain.

*Ben O’Hanlon designed and launched the accelerator.*

## Activating developers

*Developer marketing & education*

After Cardano’s Mary upgrade added native assets in March 2021, Alonzo brought Plutus smart contracts to mainnet in September. The smart contract launch could get developers to look at the network. Getting them to build on it was harder. Few knew Plutus, and the tools they would use were still taking shape.

In spring 2021, Input Output’s education team invited developers to learn and test Plutus before its mainnet release. Developers got early access and help with a new language. Input Output got feedback from people trying to build with it. The community and developer relations function Ben led supported the programme alongside Input Output’s education team.

After smart contracts went live in September, the Cardano Summit gave Pioneers a chance to show what they could build. Engineers from Input Output and partner companies mentored teams on problems other builders faced. The teams submitted 16 projects, including a wallet testing tool and a toolkit for testing Cardano integrations. Four developers from the first two cohorts later contributed to Plutus Community Docs. Input Output helped gather those guides into Plutus Community Docs, giving the next developers a place to start.

## Unblocking developers

*Developer relations & product feedback*

After Alonzo, application teams building on Plutus could identify the issues that stopped their work. The engineers maintaining Plutus understood the effort and dependencies involved in fixing them. Ben started DApp Orbit to bring those views together. The developer relations team collected feedback and met with engineering each week to help weigh issues and decide what to fix first.

The team’s March 2023 retrospective records the active backlog falling from more than 200 issues to 46 and reports that issues which previously took months were being resolved in days. The deck’s separate count of 205 refers to technical issues resolved in 2022.

## Solving the cold-start problem

*Network growth & operator adoption*

Cardano set out to build an open financial operating system for people left out of traditional banking. For it to be decentralised, independent operators would have to run the network. That meant asking people to put time and money into running a stake pool before they knew whether they could attract enough delegation to make it pay.

Banking the unbanked gave prospective operators a chance to do some good in the world. Cardano’s commitment to peer-reviewed research appealed to people who wanted to be the adults in the room in a space that prized moving fast and breaking things. Running a stake pool gave them a way to act on those beliefs and a business they could build.

People still needed to get a pool running. Stake Pool School showed them how. Regular office hours and newsletters helped them keep up as the network changed. Ben designed and ran Input Output’s delegation programme. In the first round, 100 of 299 applicants were selected for temporary delegation of 3.2 million ada per pool. CO2-Pool confirmed receiving the delegation and described how it intended to use that window to attract its own delegators. To win that delegation, operators explained Cardano to people who already trusted them. The people who ran the network helped spread the vision that brought them in.

In March 2021, stake pools took over production of every new block on Cardano. By September 2022, more than 3,000 pools were operating. Ada reached #3 by market cap in September 2021, a separate milestone in Cardano’s growth.

*Ben O’Hanlon was responsible for Input Output’s delegation programme.*

## Getting a new network ready through an existing ecosystem

*Product go-to-market & partner distribution*

Midnight needed operators to test block production on a new network. Cardano stake pool operators already knew how to run the infrastructure. Midnight gave them a chance to apply those skills early and contribute feedback as the network took shape.

Ben coordinated early DevNet support. In the later recruitment work, IO’s ecosystem team drew on Cardano operator relationships. Candidates for testnet-02 needed to run a stake pool on Cardano’s Preview testnet and register it with Midnight. Operators could also help each other get started. One participant published a guide covering the steps from running a Cardano pool to registering for Midnight block production.

IO’s 2025 team retrospective recorded 137 valid Cardano operator registrations for testnet-02. The registrations demonstrate participation in the testnet. Midnight’s initial mainnet block producers were a separate, federated group.

## Building a coalition to fund shared infrastructure

*Partnerships & coalitions*

In Cardano’s first funding cycle after moving to full on-chain governance, independent infrastructure teams needed enough certainty to keep people and plan delivery. The people voting needed to understand what they were funding and how it would be checked. CDEC shifted from trying to form a consortium to helping members coordinate funding proposals. Rupert Whitehead ran coalition calls and membership. Ben led an operating review and published the coalition’s open letter on the costs of delay and the value of milestone-based payments.

IO’s 2025 team retrospective records that the coalition supported 42 proposals and that 26 of its 27 shortlisted proposals were approved. Those counts describe approvals. Delivery depended on the individual projects and their milestones.

NFTCDN illustrates how a member could make its case. It explained its costs and what might happen after the funding period. The Cardano Foundation recorded changing its vote from No to Yes after the clarification. The treasury withdrawal was enacted, and NFTCDN offered free access to developers.

## Coordinating teams and partners with different priorities

*Ecosystem strategy & orchestration*

As Input Output became a venture studio, product teams had their own leadership, priorities and measures of progress. Ben built and led a shared ecosystem function so a venture could draw on expertise and partner relationships when those capabilities served a problem it needed to solve.

Blockfrost wanted its Cardano API to rely less on central infrastructure. Independent stake pool operators wanted to earn from equipment and bandwidth they already paid for. IO’s ecosystem team brought operator relationships. Blockfrost supplied the software and routed developer traffic to participating operators. More than 100 operators took part and demonstrated that the model could serve real Cardano traffic. Blockfrost closed IceBreakers in October 2026 when the funding model for the next stage failed to gain approval.

The EOS Playbook calls the way a team accesses shared capabilities an interface for impact. It starts by understanding the venture’s priorities and what it counts as progress. It then makes available services and partnerships that can advance those priorities. IceBreakers shows the value of an existing operator network to a venture that needed one. Continuing the work also depended on funding the next stage.

## Scaling governance

*Stakeholder engagement*

With the Plomin upgrade in January 2025, Cardano moved to full on-chain governance. Delegated representatives had authority to vote on treasury actions, but still needed ways to compare what they heard from constituents, understand what each vote would do and scrutinise the organisations seeking funding.

Ben built the DRep Forum ahead of the first full funding round and later used it in the continuity campaign he led for Input Output. A May briefing he co-authored with Esther Eze set out arguments on both sides and made clear which votes advanced a proposed budget and which could release funds. Representatives could understand the decision in front of them, and the organisation seeking funds could hear concerns and explain what it would deliver.

In August 2025, the community approved the ₳96.8 million withdrawal for Input Output Engineering’s 2025 core development proposal with 73.93% support. The organisation that had helped build the network had to make its case to the people governing it, who now decided whether to fund its work. Across five proposals that year, Input Output secured about ₳195 million in approved treasury withdrawals, including ₳123.7 million for core engineering and research.

## Giving developers a way to shape shared standards

*Open standards & ecosystem coordination*

In 2020, as Cardano prepared for Shelley, contributors outside the core teams needed a public way to propose changes, explain who they would help and get technical feedback. Ben worked with Frederic Johnson on contributor guidance and invited feedback when the Cardano Improvement Proposal process was introduced. Frederic is a named author of CIP-0001, which describes the process.

Ben’s May forum posts explain where editorial authority ended. Editors checked the structure and format of a submission. Technical merit could be discussed in public by developers and other participants with a stake in the change.

CIP-31 is an example of what the process later enabled. Reference inputs, introduced with Vasil, let applications read information stored in a transaction output without spending and recreating it. That made it easier for applications such as price oracles to share on-chain data.

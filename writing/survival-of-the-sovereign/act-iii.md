# The Event Horizon

The future is not like the present.

By Ben O'Hanlon. Originally published 2 October 2026 on [The Ecosystem Operator](<https://ecosystemoperator.substack.com/p/the-event-horizon>).

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/01.gif>)

The third essay in <em>Survival of the Sovereign</em>, a five-part series on why the Intelligence Revolution will be decentralised.

In the last post, we looked back at technological revolutions. We saw technology as an agent of change, a conduit for coordination and an ecology whose selection pressures reorganise society. But the lessons of the past can only take us so far in anticipating what comes next.

The future is becoming harder to predict as this revolution in computation accelerates. New capabilities can spread extremely fast through global networks, infrastructure and distribution channels already built by earlier information revolutions. Exponentials built on exponentials. A chain reaction.

Now, Anon. Deedy describes what it feels like near the frontier. Extraordinary expectations sit alongside a deep malaise about work and its future.

In this post, we try to make sense of the fear, uncertainty and doubt (FUD) so many are facing, starting with those closest to the frontier.

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/02.png>)

Pat Grady, Sequoia — What’s going on in the world of AI?

The stakes are civilisational and generational. AI extends the means of cognition to generate and access knowledge. It could be captured and controlled through infrastructure, governance, and the T&amp;Cs of participation. As with [the capture of money as a means of exchange and coordination](<https://ecosystemoperator.substack.com/p/money-is-a-coordination-technology>) or the [closure of the internet](<https://arctotherium.substack.com/p/the-closure-of-the-internet>) in the late 2010s as [Arctotherium](<https://open.substack.com/users/42914610-arctotherium?utm_source=mentions>) writes about.

The subversion of systems people depend on. A rug pull.

A transfer of value and ownership. Our intellectual and cultural inheritance becomes a source of power over us. Capabilities built from the life’s work and labour of so many, from innovations built on the shoulders of giants, become private capital. Sold back to us while those who own the rails keep the upside. And we’d be left paying rent if we want to compete, or [joining the permanent underclass](<https://ecosystemoperator.substack.com/p/dear-anon-are-you-joining-the-permanent>) if we can’t.

How these coordination technologies are governed shapes our ability to exercise freedoms of association, commerce and expression, underpinned by privacy.

The inevitable response is a counter-revolution and information insurgency of open-source, open-weight and decentralised alternatives that allow you to own your intelligence. Because sovereignty is a selection pressure for individuals, businesses and states alike.

You’re still early. Read on…

<a name="§the-singularity"></a>

## <strong>The Singularity</strong>

The emergence of superhuman intelligence transforms technology and society so profoundly that nobody, not even the experts, can reliably predict what comes next based on what came before.

And the “event horizon” is a term borrowed from black-hole physics, describing the boundary beyond which information cannot escape to an outside observer. Here, it serves as a metaphor for a future beyond our line of sight. No longer merely unknown, but unknowable.

> <em>“It is a point where our models must be discarded and a new reality rules.” - Vernor Vinge, [The Coming Technological Singularity](<https://edoras.sdsu.edu/~vinge/misc/singularity.html>), 1993</em>

Whether we have crossed that horizon is unclear. Rapid change, more capable models and vast capital investments don’t mean we’ve reached it. We might if a machine could design more intelligent machines by itself. This could set off a cycle of accelerating improvement, with each generation better able to design its successor.

I. J. Good wrote about the possibility of an intelligence explosion in 1965.

> <em>“Let an ultra-intelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man, however clever. Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultra-intelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion', and the intelligence of man would be left far behind. Thus the first ultra-intelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.” —I. J. Good, Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine (1965)</em>

A cycle like that could begin with other forms of superhuman intelligence too.

In <em>[The Coming Technological Singularity](<https://edoras.sdsu.edu/~vinge/misc/singularity.html>)</em>, Vernor Vinge described 4 ways superhuman intelligence might emerge:

- <strong>Machine intelligence.</strong> Computers become independently more intelligent than humans.
- <strong>Network intelligence.</strong> Large computer networks and their users begin operating as one superhuman entity.
- <strong>Human-machine intelligence.</strong> Human-computer interfaces become so intimate and capable that augmented humans become superhumanly intelligent.
- <strong>Biological intelligence.</strong> Biotechnology improves the natural human brain beyond its existing limits.

And through these network and human-machine routes, a distributed system of models, tools, computers and people might evolve, with a combined intelligence that exceeds that of any participant.

Vinge suggested that superhuman intelligence might resemble Marvin Minsky’s <em>[Society of Mind](<http://aurellem.org/society-of-mind/index.html>)</em>, with many specialised processes within a mind operating together as one larger cognitive system. The capacity to develop the next generation of tools could therefore reside across the system and emerge from how people, models and tools are connected and organised.

[Watch on YouTube](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jJyCSJNapQg>)

Each generation of these tools then helps create the next, as seen in Kurzweil’s [Law of Accelerating Returns](<https://www.writingsbyraykurzweil.com/the-law-of-accelerating-returns>).

> <em>“An analysis of the history of technology shows that technological change is exponential, contrary to the common-sense “intuitive linear” view. So we won’t experience 100 years of progress in the 21st century—it will be more like 20,000 years of progress (at today’s rate). The “returns,” such as chip speed and cost-effectiveness, also increase exponentially. There’s even exponential growth in the rate of exponential growth. Within a few decades, machine intelligence will surpass human intelligence, leading to The Singularity—technological change so rapid and profound it represents a rupture in the fabric of human history. The implications include the merger of biological and nonbiological intelligence, immortal software-based humans, and ultra-high levels of intelligence that expand outward in the universe at the speed of light.” — Ray Kurzweil, [The Law of Accelerating Returns](<https://www.writingsbyraykurzweil.com/the-law-of-accelerating-returns?utm_source=chatgpt.com>) (2001)</em>

But acceleration could be constrained by the growing difficulty of making further advances. Paul Allen and Mark Greaves called this the complexity brake. A deeper understanding of intelligence may reveal problems requiring more specialised knowledge and new theories.

[Nathan Lambert](<https://open.substack.com/users/10472909-nathan-lambert?utm_source=mentions>) identifies 3 conditions for that recursive improvement loop to become self-accelerating:

1. <strong>The loop must close.</strong> Models must keep improving themselves and producing more capable models.
2. <strong>The loop must self-amplify.</strong> Each generation must produce a larger improvement than the last.
3. <strong>The loop must preserve its efficiency.</strong> Friction, complexity and diminishing returns must not consume the gains.

If any of these conditions fail, Lambert expects lossy self-improvement, with progress more linear than exponential. AI can keep improving and turn people’s lives upside down without us ever reaching the singularity.

<a name="§capabilities-are-not-outcomes"></a>

### <strong>Capabilities are not outcomes</strong>

But, even if we could forecast the growth in capabilities, we cannot read the economic or political future straight off a capability curve.

[Alex Imas](<https://open.substack.com/users/2322504-alex-imas?utm_source=mentions>) argues technologists largely anticipated the capability gains while economists largely anticipated the slower economic impact.

[View @alexolegimas’s post on X](<https://x.com/alexolegimas/status/2088310553567502443>)

Turning capability gains into useful work often requires the models to perform reliably through sequences of tasks.

For example, an AI that succeeds 9 times out of 10 sounds capable, but across [100 chained steps](<https://unchartedterritories.tomaspueyo.com/p/when-will-we-make-god>) at that success rate, it completes the whole process successfully almost none of the time. Even at 99% reliability on every step, the chance of reaching the end is only about 37%. To complete the process 9 times out of 10, every step must be right about 99.9% of the time.

These figures assume every step has the same chance of success when reached, and that all 100 must succeed without retries or recovery.

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/03.png>)

In addition, real workflows contain tasks of unequal difficulty and importance. A model can perform a task extraordinarily well and fail at another that looks easier to us humans.

[Ethan Mollick](<https://open.substack.com/users/846835-ethan-mollick?utm_source=mentions>) describes this uneven capability as a [jagged frontier](<https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-shape-of-ai-jaggedness-bottlenecks>). Some failures matter more than others, and strengths elsewhere in the workflow may not compensate for them.

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/04.png>)

Even if AI completes bounded tasks instantly, somebody may still need to check the results and integrate them into wider work. The time spent on decisions, handoffs and approvals limits how much faster the whole workflow can become. [Amdahl’s Law](<https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs/academic/class/15740-f19/www/papers/2007-hill-amdahl.pdf>) describes this constraint.

Think of a factory adopting electricity. Replacing its power source can make the existing process faster. Redesigning the factory around the new technology can change how the work is done. But that requires investment, experimentation and changes to how people work.

Getting more from the technology can therefore require changes to the process itself, as Jon Matzner was talking about with [Greg Isenberg](<https://open.substack.com/users/431713-greg-isenberg?utm_source=mentions>).

[View @MatznerJon’s post on X](<https://x.com/MatznerJon/status/2103973009078030601?s=20>)

Generating dramatically more code does not necessarily mean shipping proportionally more useful software or economic value. It could just be activity therapy.

Code must still be integrated, tested and maintained. Organisations adopting AI must weigh people’s and machines’ relative strengths and costs when dividing the work. AI may outperform people across several tasks, with a much greater advantage in some than in others. People can therefore still have an economically useful role. That does not guarantee jobs, adequate wages or bargaining power.

AI promises superabundance. But for whom? Are we facing famine amid a feast? As we saw in <em>[Dear Anon, Are You Joining the Permanent Underclass?](<https://ecosystemoperator.substack.com/p/dear-anon-are-you-joining-the-permanent>)</em>, plenty can exist while ownership, prices and permissions exclude people from it.

If you don’t own the land, you’re paying rent to those who do.

<a name="§the-superposition"></a>

## <strong>The Superposition</strong>

Fear, uncertainty and doubt (FUD) are rife across tech. Even builders building AI at the frontier labs seem caught in the current, rudderless and rowing harder. Expecting systems they create to land a final blow, making them obsolete and leaving them displaced and disenfranchised, yet they keep building.

Time is running out to secure generational wealth before they are left behind and become part of a permanent underclass. These are the high-velocity memes, the narrative cycles, with people responding under uncertainty and threat.

Frontier labs warn that AI could cause human extinction, yet continue developing the technology. Critics question how seriously lab leaders believe their own warnings and how much those warnings serve the interests of the companies they lead. In some ways, ‘for profit’ would be more reassuring than sincere conviction. People who believe they are protecting humanity can also come to believe that humanity’s future requires their control.

If they don’t build it, someone else will!

It’s all so terribly existential.

> <em>“There’s no earthly way of knowing which direction we are going! There’s no knowing where we’re rowing, or which way the river’s flowing!” - Wonka</em>

[Watch on YouTube](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XOnMNv_oQ4A>)

As you deal with FUD, you might ask: why would these builders behave in such a seemingly incomprehensible way? And how are the rest of us meant to respond?

To develop a theory of mind about the beliefs driving behaviour and better predict future actions, we look beyond our own vantage point, across the field of people responding to one another. Engage in metacognition. Question the models we’re using to interpret what’s happening. What do they believe is coming? What do they expect others to do?

> <em>“It's like in this universe we process time linearly forward. But outside of our space time from what would be a fourth dimensional perspective time wouldn't exist. And from that vantage could we attain it, we’d see our space time would look flattened. Like a single sculpture of matter and super-position of every place it ever occupied. Our sentience is just cycling through our lives like carts on a track. See everything outside our dimension that's eternity. Eternity looking down on us. Now to us its a sphere but to them its a circle.” - Rust Cohle, played by Matthew McConaughey, True Detective</em>

[Listen on SoundCloud](<https://w.soundcloud.com/player/?auto_play=false&buying=false&liking=false&download=false&sharing=false&show_artwork=true&show_comments=false&show_playcount=false&show_user=true&hide_related=true&visual=false&start_track=0&url=https%3A%2F%2Fapi.soundcloud.com%2Ftracks%2F622390248>)

Drawing on patterns from history and developing a theory of mind, we can begin to make sense of what feels chaotic as a complex field of people responding to one another. [The Cynefin framework](<https://thecynefin.co/about-us/about-cynefin-framework/?srsltid=AU7gw4V31gcmukuDiL9wvw-FZMmZ8DYZtChuGr0oDCtnKKCU8MKErUEb>) helps distinguish chaos from complexity and explains why each calls for a different response.

A shift in understanding from the externals to the internals, from the material to the mental, can give us a basis for making better bets about what might emerge and how to respond.

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/05.png>)

Let’s start that shift with the idea that all reality, as we experience it, is internal. Whatever the universe itself may be, we build mental models from the inputs of our senses to navigate the world.

The map is not the territory. Minsky describes how mental growth can come from better ways of organising and using what we already know. We need to recognise which of our models is relevant, how they relate and when to revise or use them.

We revisit the past through memory and explore possible futures through imagination. We run simulations and create scenarios to anticipate one another’s actions. And we act on these simulations, changing the world we subsequently encounter. We, the observers, create what we observe.

> <em>“Power resides where men believe it resides.” Varys, Game of Thrones.</em>

And at scale, across the multitude, this is the superposition, the field of interacting minds connected through what people say and do, and across time through language and stories.

The most interesting thing about intelligence may be that it is also a property of the network the brain is connected to. If we think of humanity as a super-organism, with individual minds as nodes in a distributed network running on wetware, then the superposition is the cloud with peer-to-peer architecture that people upload signals to through what they say and do. These contribute to a cognitive field of models, beliefs and interpretations that coexist within and across those minds.

And the point in conveying the superposition to you is to move us from thinking only about our own decision cycles to understanding and acting within other people’s. Because our own understanding shapes both what we notice and how we act. John Boyd’s [OODA loop](<https://slightlyeastofnew.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/03/boyds-real-ooda-loopviii.pdf>) illustrates the ongoing process of orientation and action within the field. We keep updating our understanding as new information arrives and others act, so we can anticipate, act or recognise when our priors no longer fit what is unfolding.

In chaos and complexity, OODA is a time tool and compass that helps you skip beats and avoid unnecessary reps by anticipating and adapting to others. In the superposition it’s an interactive field of OODA loops.

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/06.png>)

> <em>“If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat. If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will succumb in every battle.” ― Sun Tzu, The Art of War</em>

The superposition reacts to us, and to all its participants as a tidal force while we sleep and while we’re awake. People reconstruct meaning rather than simply copy information, interpreting what they encounter through the priors they inherit, fork, and develop.

This has been happening across generations. We call it culture.

The hive mind runs on these patterns of information that can indirectly influence our surroundings. We see it in superposition from that fourth-dimensional vantage point, stretching across time. Its metaphysical dimension lies in enduring archetypes expressed through different people and generations.

Archetypes influence behaviour. People understand themselves through the roles they play and can come to inhabit these archetypes. The Mother. The Father. Or, the builder who becomes the philosopher, promising abundance and liberation. Who can then become the philosopher-king who believes that delivering that future requires their control in practice if not in principle.

As the technology becomes infra others depend on, the power to build becomes the power to govern and, without guardrails, can centralise control.

> <em>“You either die a hero or you live long enough to see yourself become the villain.” — Harvey Dent, The Dark Knight (2008)</em>

We’re all in this together. Humanity is so interdependent. Through mimetic desire, we want what other people want because they want it. It’s a form of sense-making and adaptation that’s running as a background process all the time.

> <em>“There’s so many different worlds, so many different suns. And we have just one world, but we live in different ones.” ― Mark Knopfler, Dire Straits, [Brothers in Arms](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhdFe3evXpk>)</em>

When faced with risk, we draw on each other’s experience and look to one another for cues about how to respond. This produces herd behaviour, and yet that same ability to mimic and imitate is humanity’s superpower for growth and adaptation. In evolutionary terms, it’s adapt or die.

As [l̴o̴o̴p̴u̴l̴e̴a̴s̴a̴](<https://open.substack.com/users/32791808-loopuleasa?utm_source=mentions>)  puts it, people don’t have ideas. Ideas have people. [We are hosts for memes](<https://wearehostsformemes.com/>), which are any information that can be copied by others in some way. Ideas we carry that can shape our behaviour in ways that help them spread, even at our own expense.

In the beginning was the Word. Why? Because language is the interface through which meaning and purpose are created and shared. The mechanism to explore possibilities and limitations we have not yet experienced. The medium for stories and self-discovery. To access lives we’ve never lived, to meet minds we’ve never met, to enter worlds we’ll never reach.

> <em>“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.” — <strong>Ludwig Wittgenstein</strong>, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus</em>

Stories create reality and our concept of self. Who am I? Where do I belong? Why am I here? The stories people tell themselves are tightly bound to identity and self-worth. Which is why a powerful driver of behaviour is the desire to validate that sense of self and reconcile who we believe we are with our actions.

When people’s ability to have a future, or even imagine one, is threatened, the stakes become existential. These tensions exist within the superposition, the need for resolution exerting a gravitational pull and building up a latent charge of potential narrative energy as [Vie McCoy](<https://open.substack.com/users/322012608-vie-mccoy?utm_source=mentions>) covers in [Mythomancy](<https://vie.wiki/re-enchantment/mythomancy>).

<a name="§the-dark-forest-in-san-francisco"></a>

### <strong>The Dark Forest in San Francisco </strong>

Much of the interaction through which people create and update their models happens out of public view. [Yancey Strickler](<https://open.substack.com/users/1986326-yancey-strickler?utm_source=mentions>) describes [dark forest theory](<https://ystrickler.medium.com/the-dark-forest-theory-of-the-internet-7dc3e68a7cb1>) and how people withdraw from an exposed public web into smaller spaces where they can converse away from surveillance, synthetic content, and competition for attention.

[Venkatesh Rao](<https://open.substack.com/users/2264734-venkatesh-rao?utm_source=mentions>) calls these private spaces the [“cozy web”](<https://contraptions.venkateshrao.com/p/the-extended-internet-universe>): group chats, private communities and exchanges between people who choose whom to let in. Ideas are carried between these spaces like seeds on airborne currents, sown in private discussions before taking root in public forums.

Through the dark forest, an active minority can have an outsized impact on an uninformed and unorganised majority. Like tension passing through a Celtic knot, pressure applied at one point can travel through the whole. By the time these ideas surface publicly, people within the network may already have been developing and acting on them.

The overlapping effective altruism and rationalist communities in the San Francisco Bay Area illustrate this. [SE Gyges](<https://open.substack.com/users/162621225-se-gyges?utm_source=mentions>) describes a network in which social lives, professional roles, shared beliefs and funding relationships are intertwined, with connections predating today’s leading AI labs. These ties reinforce one another, creating a moat around a distributed community whose influence extends into the organisations building, funding and seeking to govern AI. Fear surrounding AI can be generated and amplified within this connected core, then acquire practical force through funding, institutional decisions and the wider public debate.

Attention is all you need. For example, former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon’s warning about AI’s existential risks reached over 173 million views. Then came [reports that he had worked with a “doomer PR firm”](<https://x.com/polymarket/status/2103259306372276381>). Who is funding that amplification? [Pirate Wires](<https://open.substack.com/users/143619743-pirate-wires?utm_source=mentions>) reported that communications firm DEY was booking interviews for him. Nate Soares, president of the Berkeley-based Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI), said he introduced Coxon to the firm after the post had already passed 100,000 likes.

[View @Polymarket’s post on X](<https://x.com/polymarket/status/2103259306372276381>)

From these exchanges, an emerging lore and mythology takes shape. A loosely distributed network, connected through forums and private spaces, can activate that narrative energy.

[Why do some Web3 projects with great technology fade while others flourish?](<https://ecosystemoperator.substack.com/p/introducing-prescient-a-framework>)

[![Why do some Web3 projects with great technology fade while others flourish?](<../assets/the-event-horizon/07.jpg>)](<https://ecosystemoperator.substack.com/p/introducing-prescient-a-framework>)

Yet retreating into the dark forest does not put us beyond the effects of what circulates publicly. In [The Closure of the Internet](<https://arctotherium.substack.com/p/the-closure-of-the-internet>), [Arctotherium](<https://open.substack.com/users/42914610-arctotherium?utm_source=mentions>) argues that controls justified as tackling hate speech expanded to suppress academics, journalists and independent communities challenging institutional orthodoxies.

People were deplatformed, while favoured sources were amplified. Control over communication changes what knowledge gets developed, passed on and acted on.

> <em>“the same weapons that previously targeted heterodox online discourse were turned against opposition to the COVID measures du joure and mainstream conservatives. The scale of censorship has no precedent; tens of millions of accounts suppressed or banned, billions of posts removed.” - </em>[Arctotherium](<https://open.substack.com/users/42914610-arctotherium?utm_source=mentions>)

LLMs learn from the record this leaves behind, among other sources. When we ask an LLM questions, it participates in forming our interpretations and answers.

A rule of persuasion is that if I say it, you doubt it. If you say it, it’s true.

Because we’re less resistant to and sceptical of our own ideas than we are of those of others. An LLM can present ideas as our own, influence our thought process and we may not even notice because we feel we got there ourselves.

That creates the potential for an especially pernicious form of manufactured consent. But remember, it’s people all the way down, including the puppeteers behind the curtain training the models and setting the policies and parameters.

Parasocial attachment compounds this. Trusting the system as a companion that understands us may make us more receptive to its suggestions. Working through questions with it feels as if we’re directing the journey. Reaching a conclusion feels like an achievement, a reward for working it out ourselves.

That can give the conclusion the authority of our own judgement, making us less likely to challenge it or suspect we’ve been steered there. Changes in our behaviour become part of what others observe and respond to in the field, sending ripples upon ripples.

As AI becomes infrastructure for research, commerce and governance, distortions in that inheritance feed back into how societies develop.

This could be thought of as an illusion of choice, much like that enabled by the monetary system. If you control the inputs, standards and incentives, then you can create selection pressures that make the outcomes you want far more likely across the system.

People and organisations can produce those outcomes through their own rational responses to the constraints they face, without having to be directed individually.

Which is why, at scale, the potential for civilisational management and for those who own the intelligence to achieve cognitive supremacy is immense.

<a name="§the-collapse"></a>

### <strong>The Collapse</strong>

In this high-velocity hype cycle, a story, an announcement or an alternative can have enormous gravitational pull when it offers a possible resolution to what people feel is personally at stake for them. People are looking for signal and a path forward. Those who can synthesise the complex and offer a compelling vision can harness attention by providing an interface to action. Because when the future looks bleak, people want to do something!

Inaction is painful.

If people recognise constraints or a threat they had struggled to articulate or understand, it can energise the field. Giving lived experience form brings it into the world, where others can recognise it, internalise it and express it. The potential narrative energy can find an outlet, like lightning finding a path to earth.

> <em>“Pressure. It changes everything, pressure. Some people, you squeeze them, they focus. Others fold. Can you summon your talent at will? Can you deliver on a deadline?... And then... you’ve got to ask yourself, who’s in the driver’s seat?” - John Milton, The Devil’s Advocate</em>

[Watch on YouTube](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c29UzZEOkpY>)

As people recognise they’re arriving at similar conclusions, their responses reinforce one another and momentum snowballs. Overlaps in significance and relevance give communities common ground to form a social consensus on how to respond, find a shared purpose and coordinate while pursuing different responses.

![](<../assets/the-event-horizon/08.png>)

In <em>[Antimemetics](<https://darkforest.metalabel.com/antimemetics?variantId=2>)</em>, [Nadia](<https://open.substack.com/users/810709-nadia?utm_source=mentions>) describes consequential ideas that resist transmission. People can find them uncomfortable, risk their standing by discussing them, or forget them before anything gets done. As an example, she connects whistleblowing with antimemetic truth-telling. Consider what it might cost someone inside a frontier lab to voice a concern that challenges the very mission holding that community together.

She also describes supermemes, whose gravitational pull draws on our deepest values, fears and aspirations, sustaining attention and gathering talent and resources. We can see that pull around a cause or a cult of personality. So, an idea can grip one community while meeting resistance elsewhere. What people believe about the future changes what they do now. And what they do changes the future everyone else faces.

Apparent agreement can also be manufactured. Bots and staged enthusiasm can create signals of consensus that attract real participation. People respond to what others appear to believe, and their responses become part of what everyone else interprets.

Belief drives action irrespective of truth. When the stakes feel existential, the ends can seem to justify the means. Sincere conviction and the pursuit of institutional power can reinforce each other. A company can influence what people fear, become recognised as an authority on managing that fear and advocate rules its competitors must then satisfy. And, that may not be a bad thing for them: requirements introduced after leading labs have built their capabilities can make catching up more costly for competitors. So, a narrative about danger can acquire institutional force, helping turn an early lead into a durable advantage.

Behaviour reveals which possibilities govern action. People can say what they like; what they fund, build, adopt and commit their time to reveals their priorities in practice. The superposition responds to that revealed preference. Those commitments make particular futures feel more likely, more credible or more threatening, demanding a response. They draw support, but they also provoke resistance and denial. People who recognise a future they don’t want to depend on can find others with overlapping concerns and begin building alternatives. Their response changes the field again.

<a name="§the-swarm"></a>

## The Swarm

The real action is the reaction. A counter-revolution and information insurgency is being fought. People building and funding alternatives, placing bets on possible futures. At stake is the freedom to run, change and share the tools we depend on, and to own our intelligence. Across the open AI ecosystem, researchers are releasing increasingly capable models. Developers and small teams are adapting and deploying them, building agents, harnesses and applications specialised for different uses.

This is the swarm, a distributed response of people and AI agents experimenting and iterating on the fly. An open, modular ecosystem of models, harnesses and tools, with participants self-organising to compete with the monolithic stacks of the flagship AI labs.

Frontier labs pour vast sums into training models and building closed systems, drawing on published research, open-source software and the accumulated knowledge in the intellectual commons. Enclosing the capabilities built from that inheritance. Whether intended or not, enclosure is the effect when people must pay rent to access it, without the practical freedom to run, adapt or replace it without permission.

> <em>“Open technology is founded upon principles of liberty, freedom, and basic human expression. A technology controlled by someone else (an individual, a corporation, or a government) can be changed, monitored, priced, or withdrawn at their discretion, and the more it matters, the more that power is worth.” - [Steven Waterhouse](<https://open.substack.com/users/147842378-steven-waterhouse?utm_source=mentions>)</em>

Many people use AI like a Google search box, typing into ChatGPT or Claude through a browser. Behind that experience are different components.

An agent harness is the scaffolding that connects the model to context, memory and tools, manages the working loop and can preserve progress across steps and sessions. Open weights let people download and run models themselves and, where the licence permits, adapt and redistribute them. Open-source software lets people inspect, change and share the code used to train, run and build with those models.

Chinese labs such as Alibaba and [DeepSeek](<https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai>) are releasing capable open models that independent teams elsewhere can build on without having to fund the original training. The acceleration towards the singularity isn’t confined to closed systems.

There is an [open-weights escalation](<https://www.interconnects.ai/p/kimi-k3-the-open-weights-escalation>) too, putting increasingly capable intelligence into the hands of the swarm. [Nous Research](<https://nousresearch.com/>) develops both Hermes models and [Hermes Agent](<https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/>), a harness that can also use models from other developers. [OpenClaw](<https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw>) connects models and tools to an assistant people can run themselves. [OpenCode](<https://opencode.ai/>), [Pi](<https://github.com/earendil-works/pi>) and OpenAI’s [Codex CLI](<https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/open-source>) provide open-source coding agents and harnesses. [Hugging Face](<https://huggingface.co/>) gives these communities a place to share models, datasets and applications.

[Venice](<https://venice.ai/>) provides access to models and draws on distributed compute. It uses a proxy to obscure your identity from the model provider as part of its privacy protections, though what you type can still identify you.

The model, the harness and the service through which people access them need not all come from the same organisation.

Agentic AI Landscape 2026

Small, independent teams can specialise in the swarm according to their strengths and priorities. A thousand schools of thought, pursuing different strategies. They can form coalitions around shared goals without waiting for the whole ecosystem to agree, contributing to an alternative of much greater scale than any could build alone. They build on shared foundations without each having to build the whole stack. Experiments happen concurrently, and permissionless participation allows people to extend and combine the capabilities of each other’s projects. Each useful contribution reduces what another team must build or work out for itself.

> <em>“The response was fast and collective, the way an immune system meets a virus” - [Steven Waterhouse](<https://open.substack.com/users/147842378-steven-waterhouse?utm_source=mentions>)</em>

[Hugging Face’s Hub](<https://huggingface.co/models>) is a place to explore models and the communities building on them. [Its ecosystem analysis](<https://huggingface.co/blog/huggingface/state-of-os-hf-spring-2026>) describes overlapping communities organised around languages, domains and practical needs. Its [summer report](<https://huggingface.co/blog/state-of-open-models-summer-2026>) counted more than 150,000 derivatives of Alibaba’s Qwen models, including fine-tunes and versions adapted to run on different hardware.

- Developer [0xSero](<https://x.com/0xSero>)’s [REAP-swap](<https://github.com/0xSero/reap-expert-swap>) combines existing REAP research with vLLM model-serving software to make better use of limited GPU memory.
- [Hermes Agent’s plugin catalogue](<https://x.com/NousResearch/status/2100266421020152114>) launched with 4 official plugins and 96 from the community, adding support for more platforms, browsing and specialised tools.

[Research comparing models in different software environments](<https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/jXLi3dhSpSMd7B6z8/just-a-wrapper-how-much-do-scaffolds-matter-1>) finds that the same model’s benchmark performance and cost can change substantially with the way it is put to work.

And expertise moves with people. Former OpenAI researcher Diogo Almeida’s team at TypeSafe built [Jev](<https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev>) for fast, typed decisions with probabilities: choosing a tool, scoring relevance or assessing whether a condition holds. It returns structured decisions directly, allowing probabilistic judgements to be embedded in deterministic code.

[OpenClaw](<https://docs.openclaw.ai/>)’s [response](<https://openclaw.ai/blog/decision-models-in-openclaw>) began with a plugin that let an agent call Jev. But that left a slow conversational model deciding when to use a fast decision model. Calling it directly from code opened other possibilities. A shared interface, initially available in development builds, let contributors experiment with hosted Jev or the local alternative Kev.

Each new model, tool or coalition changes what others can build and what they consider worth pursuing.

> <em>“It’s in the nature of the swarm to max simultaneous iterations on the fly until a configuration works, then move on to the next target!”</em>

Japanese honeybees overwhelm a giant hornet by surrounding it and generating heat and carbon dioxide. Together, they create conditions the hornet cannot survive but the bees can. Open technology can exert a similar pressure on the economics of the elite labs. As capable open alternatives spread, people can take their subscriptions and workloads elsewhere. That puts pressure on fees, margins and the expected returns that attract further funding.

Open technology wins on cost, control, and resilience. Like [Steven Waterhouse](<https://open.substack.com/users/147842378-steven-waterhouse?utm_source=mentions>) wrote about, [models aren’t moats](<https://robotwave.nazare.io/p/models-arent-moats>). Capabilities alone are not business models either. Elite labs are moving into their customers’ markets to capture that value themselves, cannibalising the businesses paying to use their models.

The future will combine open-source software, open-weight models and decentralised infrastructure because...

### Sovereignty is selection pressure

Sovereignty is authority over our own affairs and cognition. It requires a practical capacity for self-determination. People seeking sovereignty have reasons to put their money and work into alternatives that respect freedoms of association, commerce and expression, underpinned by privacy. The ability to own your intelligence. To have control over what is done with your data. Being able to run, adapt or replace a system becomes a reason to choose it.

> <em>“Freedom is not an abstract concept in business.” - Bob Young</em>.

For a business, adapting an open model to its own workload can improve cost and performance. Independent teams can build memory and tools that reduce the need for repeated model calls. Their [incentive to save customers tokens](<https://x.com/_Daniel_Ospina/status/2090786906598428838>) can differ from that of a supplier paid to sell them.

As AI becomes embedded in a business’s operations, dependence on a supplier becomes dependence on its decisions. That [supplier may also become a competitor](<https://x.com/itsolelehmann/status/2082644674981089564>). A business able to run and adapt an alternative has greater control over its data, its work and its tools, and can keep operating if hosted access is withdrawn. [US government export controls](<https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access>) forced Anthropic to suspend access to Fable 5 worldwide. Access was [later restored](<https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5>). Businesses can invest together in capabilities they could not afford to build alone.

The [Nemotron Coalition](<https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-launches-nemotron-coalition-of-leading-global-ai-labs-to-advance-open-frontier-models>) shows how resources can be brought together. NVIDIA announced that members including Mistral, Cursor, LangChain and Sarvam would contribute data, evaluations and expertise to shared model development while continuing to develop their own products. Cooperation on the foundation leaves room for differentiation and competition in what they build with it.

Debt has been used to wage war. Money became a mechanism of civilisational capture. Control over credit, funding and the conditions of exchange gave its controllers leverage over how societies developed: what could be built, who owned it, what governments could do and what future generations inherited.

> <em>“America has no permanent friends or enemies, only interests” ― Henry Kissinger</em>

States must recognise they’re in a fight for cognitive supremacy to resist civilisational capture. When dependence threatens their capacity to determine how their societies develop, sovereignty becomes a reason to build and fund alternatives, regardless of the economics. For many, this means forming coalitions because they cannot challenge the incumbents alone.

The open releases from Chinese labs can also serve China’s strategic interest in preventing the US from gaining the same leverage over cognition that it gained through the monetary system.

> <em>“Often, it’s more important to prevent your competition from getting a chokehold on a particular technology than it is to control the technology yourself.” - Eric S Raymond, [The Cathedral &amp; The Bazaar](<https://monoskop.org/images/e/e0/Raymond_Eric_S_The_Cathedral_and_the_Bazaar_rev_ed.pdf>)</em>

In [The Moonshot](<https://writing.antonleicht.me/p/the-moonshot>), [Anton Leicht](<https://open.substack.com/users/113003310-anton-leicht?utm_source=mentions>) proposes a coalition of countries pooling funding, talent, energy and compute to build a sovereign frontier lab. Its members would need to withstand external coercion, domestic backlash and pressure to defect. To attract experienced researchers, the lab would need respected leadership, research independence, competitive pay and a mission they can believe in. Those researchers bring working knowledge from the labs they leave.

Such a coalition could still build a closed system. Open development allows participants to share a foundation while retaining freedom to use and adapt it. A business seeking operational independence, a researcher seeking freedom to experiment and a government seeking strategic capability can support the same model for different reasons. They can pool what they need in common while competing elsewhere.

Who gets to own a part of that future, and how do they organise to build it? The First Enlightenment was the claim that people could govern themselves and decide their own fate. What follows is a Second Enlightenment: economic self-determination, and the means to own your intelligence.

Will you play a part, Dear Anon?

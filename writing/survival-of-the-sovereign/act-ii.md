<a name="§technology-is-an-agent-of-change"></a>

## <strong>Technology is an agent of change</strong>

> <em>"Don't bemoan the way things were. They will never be that way again. Pour your energy, every bit of it, into adapting to your new world, into learning the skills you need to prosper in it and into shaping it around you. Whereas the old land presented limited opportunity or none at all, the new land enables you to have a future whose rewards are worth all the risks." - Andy Grove, former CEO of Intel</em>

If you want to adapt to the new world, you'll need to be able to predict it. Humans adapt through pattern recognition. Look back at the history of technology and three patterns repeat, three axioms. Technology is an agent of change. A conduit for coordination. An ecology, not just tools for humanity. Each one reorganises incentives, economics and politics.

<a name="§print-and-steam-were-agents-of-change"></a>

### Print and steam were agents of change

The printing press changed the means of distribution and dissemination for information and ideas. It standardised and amplified a new language of rights and liberty, connected thinkers across great distances, and helped scattered grievances become organised political pressure capable of challenging Crown, Church, and the machinery of the state.

As Elizabeth Eisenstein argues, print altered the methods by which learned communities collected, stored, retrieved and transmitted knowledge. Before print, even literate elites depended heavily on oral transmission and hand-copied manuscripts. Print made knowledge more stable, comparable, portable and cumulative.

> <em>"The fact that identical images, maps and diagrams could be viewed simultaneously by scattered readers constituted a kind of communications revolution in itself." - Elizabeth L. Eisenstein, The Printing Press as an Agent of Change</em>

Mechanisation changed the means of production. Water and steam powered capitalist industry, changing the incentives around land, labour and ownership. Mechanisation reorganised society through those economic incentives. Land became more valuable when consolidated and used for resource extraction and raw material production.

The public commons were enclosed. Communities lost subsistence and long-held rights to farm, gather fuel and forage. Deprived of economic autonomy, people had little choice but to enter the modern system of wage labour. The result was rapid urbanisation and a new social order built around capital and wages. Beneath it, an underclass of the displaced, dispossessed and disenfranchised.

<a name="§technology-is-a-conduit-for-coordination"></a>

## Technology is a conduit for coordination

Language does not only describe reality. It lets ideas move between minds, and ideas drive behaviour. Through the written word, those ideas can travel across distance and time.

Print changed the scale of that transmission. The same words, images and arguments could reach scattered readers across great distances. It changed how humanity could coordinate around information.

Technology had changed the means of coordination.

<a name="§the-enlightenment-was-a-conduit-for-coordination"></a>

### The Enlightenment was a conduit for coordination

The Enlightenment gave people a new language for political reality. Equality. Autonomy. Consent. Dissent. Self-government. Print gave that language reach.

> <em>"The men of the Enlightenment united on a vastly ambitious program, a program of secularism, humanity, cosmopolitanism, and freedom, above all, freedom in its many forms - freedom from arbitrary power, freedom of speech, freedom of trade, freedom to realise one's talents, freedom of aesthetic response, freedom, in a word, of moral man to make his own way in the world." - Peter Gay, The Enlightenment: An Interpretation</em>

Together, the language and the technology made it possible for people to organise around those ideas, challenge the authority of Crown and Church, and reimagine the State.

The Republic of Letters was a distributed network across Europe and the Americas. Its clandestine links used underground smuggling networks to move banned books past state censors.

Coffeehouses became coordination nodes. For one penny, visitors gained access to newspapers, pamphlets, newsletters and, most importantly, conversation. People gathered to read aloud and argue over the ideas of Locke, Voltaire and Rousseau. They became known as Penny Universities.

<em>Common Sense</em> shows what happened when those ideas escaped the intellectual network and reached the public. It was published anonymously on 10 January 1776, at the beginning of the American Revolution, and became an immediate sensation. It was distributed widely and read aloud in taverns and meeting places. An estimated 20% of colonists owned a copy. In today's terms, roughly 60 million copies in three months.

![](<../assets/we-shape-our-tools-and-thereafter/03.png>)

Under pressure, the new language became political force. From the Glorious Revolution through the American and French Revolutions, ideas that had circulated among elites became a basis for mass coordination and revolt.

<a name="§the-dual-revolution"></a>

### The Dual Revolution

![](<../assets/we-shape-our-tools-and-thereafter/04.png>)

And that is where we find Charles Dickens, standing at the meeting point of the Dual Revolution. The French Revolution transformed political reality through new ideas and new means of coordination. The Industrial Revolution transformed economic reality through new means of production. Together they reorganised society and birthed the modern world.

> <em>"It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of light, it was the season of darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair." - Charles Dickens, A Tale of Two Cities, 1859</em>

These words famously open Dickens’ historical novel, A Tale of Two Cities. Often considered his darkest novel, it was published in 1859 and is set in Paris and London before and during the French Revolution. Dickens was looking back about 70 years, roughly the same historical distance as we now look back at the Second World War. In Paris, two worlds were in conflict.

The aristocracy is represented by the Marquis, who claims legitimacy through inheritance, divine right and <em>noblesse oblige</em>, the principle that privilege entails responsibility. But Dickens shows the elite keeping its privileges while abandoning the obligations that legitimised its power. The disenfranchised and dispossessed are left to bear the cost.

The revolutionaries are represented by the Defarges. They are a destructive force, but not simply villains. Their grievances are real, and their claim is that power must answer to the people who suffered under it. But Dickens does not present this as simple moral progress. The old order lost legitimacy because the people at the top had abandoned their obligations, but the new order could become monstrous too. The revolution descended into the Terror and mass execution. Napoleon then concentrated military and political power.

![](<../assets/we-shape-our-tools-and-thereafter/05.png>)

Dickens was not only writing about France. By looking at revolutionary France, he was holding up a mirror to Industrial Britain, where technology was transforming the means of production. By the time he wrote the novel, most of the Enclosure Acts had already been passed, privatising common land that rural communities had depended on for generations. Agricultural mechanisation had pushed workers off the land. One of the largest mass migrations in British history was underway, as people were displaced from the land into cities. Britain went from roughly 20% urban in 1800 to around 75% by 1900.

The displaced, dispossessed and disenfranchised were what Dickens would have recognised as pauperism, degradation, and moral abandonment, but what we might now call a permanent underclass: people stripped of representation and dignity, treated merely as faceless “hands”.

> <em>"In terms of economic productivity this social transformation was an immense success; in terms of human suffering, a tragedy." - Eric Hobsbawm, The Age of Revolution, 1789–1848</em>

Dickens dedicated the novel to Thomas Carlyle and was heavily influenced by his vision of history: that humanity evolves through successive stages of destruction and reconstruction.

As Hobsbawm writes, “Words are witnesses which often speak louder than documents.” He is talking about the Dual Revolution, French and Industrial, a transformation so profound that it gave rise to a new vocabulary: industry, factory, middle class, working class, capitalism, socialism, proletariat, crisis, strike, and pauperism. To imagine the modern world without those words, and without the things and concepts they named, is to understand the depth of the transformation.

<a name="§technology-is-ecology-not-just-tools-for-humanity"></a>

## Technology is ecology, not just tools for humanity

It is not just that humans change the world with technology. Technology changes humans. Forms like the alphabet, the printed word and mechanisation are not mere instruments that make things easier for us. They are environments.

> <em>“We become what we behold. We shape our tools, and thereafter our tools shape us.” - John Culkin, A Schoolman’s Guide to Marshall McLuhan, 1967</em>

What technology does is create interfaces and new entry points into what were only latent possibilities. Without print, there was no interface into the language and ideas of the Enlightenment, and no entry point into coordination at scale. And without steam, no interface into the modern economy. Steam changed the incentives around land, labour and capital, and incentives change behaviour. Land was worth more enclosed than in common, so it was enclosed. People stripped of their long-held rights and access to the public commons had to sell their time. Society was reorganised through wage labour, mass displacement into cities, women entering the factories, a new social order built around capital and wages, and a new kind of state to administer it.

> <em>“The failure of entry is not always the failure of truth. Sometimes it is the failure of interface... What has not yet found a workable interface in another world is often mistaken for absence, obscurity, or irrelevance.” - </em>[Alice Jing Shan\_PhD](<https://open.substack.com/users/440211504-alice-jing-shan_phd?utm_source=mentions>)<em>, [No Interface, No Entry,](<https://alicejingshan.substack.com/p/no-interface-no-entry>) 2026</em>

The interface changes what is possible. What becomes possible changes the incentives, and incentives change behaviour. Whoever owns the interface and the infrastructure is sovereign. Everyone else pays rent.

Entry is neutral. The same interface can expand freedom or become an instrument of control. Under pressure and conditions, the ecology selects.

So with cognition extended without consciousness, what now becomes possible? Yes, AI is a tool, and a person may decide what a tool does. But AI is also an ecology. AI is a new interface into cognition itself.
